How VivOME works, start to end

VivOME looks at the proteins measured in a single cell and says what kind of blood cell it is, or says plainly that it cannot tell, and why. This page shows how, one step at a time, following one real cell. You do not need a background in biology or computing: every term is explained as it comes.

The question VivOME answers

Four ideas, one picture each. Everything after this builds on them.

  1. 01

    Every cell has a type

    Blood holds many kinds of cells: T cells and B cells that fight infection, red cells that carry oxygen, platelets that help it clot, and more. Knowing which kind a cell is comes first in almost any study of it.

  2. 02

    Two ways to read a cell

    Every cell carries the same genes but switches different ones on. A switched-on gene is copied into RNA, a working message, which is used to build proteins, the molecules that do the cell's work. Measure either one, and you learn something about the cell.

  3. 03

    One side comes with names, the other does not

    Experts have named the type of tens of thousands of cells measured by their RNA. Protein measurements of single cells are newer: they catch only the more plentiful proteins, and they rarely come with names for the cells.

  4. 04

    VivOME's idea

    Learn what each type looks like from the named RNA cells, and draw them as a map. Then place a protein measurement on that map, through the genes both sides share, and name it. When the evidence is too weak to name a type, say so, and say why.

    Two kinds of data

    In plain words

    Every cell in the body carries the same genes, but each kind of cell switches on a different set. A switched-on gene is copied into RNA, a working message, and the RNA is used to build proteins, the molecules that do the cell's work. So reading a cell's RNA shows which genes are on, and reading its proteins shows what it has built. Both carry the gene's name, which is how VivOME lines them up.

    The cell types fall into a few broad branches. In the lymphoid branch, T cells direct the immune response or kill infected cells, NK cells kill infected or abnormal cells, and B cells and plasma cells make antibodies. In the myeloid branch, monocytes patrol the blood and become macrophages that swallow germs, dendritic cells show pieces of germs to T cells, and neutrophils are first to arrive at an infection. Red blood cells carry oxygen, stem and precursor cells make new blood cells, and platelets help blood clot.

    Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 2 of 4

    What a protein file looks like

    In plain words

    Where the file comes from. A mass spectrometer is an instrument that breaks a cell's proteins into small pieces and weighs them very precisely; what it writes out is a raw file of signal readings, one per sample, and here each sample is one cell. DIA-NN is free software that reads those raw files and works out which proteins were there and how much of each. (Its name comes from the way the instrument is run, called data-independent acquisition or DIA, and the neural networks, NN, it uses.) Its report is the table above: a few columns describing each protein ( ), then one column per raw file, holding that protein's amount in that cell, or a blank.

    Why so many blanks. The instrument catches the most plentiful proteins in each cell and misses rarer ones, so most of the table is empty. Sometimes it cannot tell very similar proteins apart and reports them together as one protein group, which is why some names are joined with ";". Each column is named after its raw file, such as  ; VivOME keeps just the cell's name.

    One shared list of genes

    In plain words

    A gene's name, such as CD3E, is shared by the gene, its RNA and its protein, which is how the two sides can be lined up. The protein side is much shorter because the instrument sees only the most plentiful proteins in a cell, so VivOME can compare only the genes both sides know. Names come in versions too: most files use a gene's official name, but some use an older name or a nickname, so VivOME translates them first.     Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 4 of 4

    How VivOME learns

    In plain words

    Learning here works like learning faces from labelled photos: after seeing enough named examples, you can name a face you have never seen. VivOME does the same with cells, learning what each type tends to look like. Its map is how it holds that knowledge: each cell becomes a point, and similar cells sit close together.

    Hiding genes on purpose is like practising to recognise a song from a few notes: it prepares VivOME for protein files, where most genes are missing. Practice batches also come in different mixes, from a natural blood sample to a single sorted kind of cell, because real files come both ways. A random start is simply where a learner begins before it has learned anything; different starts end in slightly different learners, which is why several are averaged.

    For the curious: the full training recipe

    Under the hood, four rules guide each adjustment: pull each cell toward its type, pull cells of one type together, stop any single point becoming everyone's nearest neighbour, and make the two gapped views of one cell land in the same place.

    The learners

    Three ways of practising, compared

    Before settling on this design, three ways of hiding genes during practice were tried. Each was trained   times and tested the same way. All three scored about the same, so the choice mattered little; the first was kept.

    Part 2 · Reading a new file · Step 1 of 2

    Fitting the file to the shared list

    In plain words

    An empty seat is not a zero. Think of a form where a blank box is marked "not answered" instead of being filled in with 0: the mask is that mark, so VivOME never mistakes "not measured" for "none".

    Why it matters: without the mask, every empty seat would have to hold some number, say 0. After the last step below, 0 means "exactly average", so most of our cell would look perfectly average, and two very different cells could look alike. With the mask, VivOME reads only the seats that were measured and sets the rest aside. It learned to do this in Part 1, step 4, where genes were hidden on purpose and the mask said which.

    Comparing within the file works like grading on a curve. Each cell's amount of a gene is read as above or below the file's typical cell, so a machine that reads everything brighter gives the same picture as one that reads dimmer.

    For the curious: the numbers behind it

    For each gene, the file's values are shifted and stretched so that their average is 0 and their spread is 1 (a z-score). An empty seat is set to 0 with its mask 0, so the model can tell an empty seat from an average one. The minimum of   genes is provisional.

    Part 2 · Reading a new file · Step 2 of 2

    Borrowing from similar cells

    In plain words

    It is like asking a few people who saw the same thing: one may misremember a detail, but several similar accounts steady the story. VivOME borrows only from cells in the same file, and only from the ones most like our cell, so a cell is never blended with cells of a different kind.

    For the curious: how the matches are found

    The matches are found from all of the file's proteins, not only the shared genes, in a summary of   numbers per cell. Each blended value is   the cell's own and   its matches' weighted average; the weights favour the closest matches. The table under the figure lists our cell's matches and their weights.

    Part 3 · The model's judgement · Step 2 of 6

    Which types is our cell close to?

    In plain words

    It is like asking five people to point to where a stranger is from: each points a little differently, and the average of their answers is steadier than any single one.

    For the curious: closeness and chances

    Closeness is the cosine between the cell's point and each type's centre. Each learner turns its cosines into chances with a softmax at its own sharpness (its temperature), fitted on RNA cells it had not trained on. The table under the figure lists the average chance for every type.

    Part 3 · The model's judgement · Step 3 of 6

    A second opinion

    In plain words

    A second opinion is like checking a hunch against a reference book. The learners give the hunch: they have seen many named example cells and know roughly where ours belongs. The book holds each type's fingerprint, which genes it has more or less of. VivOME reads our cell against every page and sees which fits best.

    The two help each other because they work differently: the learners judge from what they learned, the book compares genes directly. But the book only works when the file holds several kinds of cell, so VivOME first checks whether book and hunch mostly agree across the file, and only then listens to the book.

    For the curious: how the match score is worked out

    The match score is a correlation (Pearson's r) over the genes our cell measured. Our cell's side is each gene compared within the file: how far above or below the file's average cell, in the file's own spread. The type's side is its fingerprint: the average of its example cells, every type weighted equally, compared with all example cells the same way. The scores become chances with a softmax at a temperature of  : the smaller the temperature, the more the best-matching types take.

    The figures from testing are development figures. On Fulcher 2026 every type is counted equally; that file also chose how much weight the second opinion gets, so it is not an independent test. On the   practice files the best guess named   correctly with the second opinion and   without it; on files of one kind of cell it does not help, which is why it is checked first.

    The best guess

    In plain words

    The best guess is what VivOME would say if it had to pick one type. It is always given, but it is only a guess: the next step decides whether VivOME is sure enough to give a real answer. Mixing in the second opinion, where the file allows it, makes the guess right more often.

    For the curious: how the guess is made

    The best guess comes from a newer set of learners than the answer in the next step, so its chances differ a little from those in step 2.     The answer, or the reason for none

    In plain words

    VivOME would rather say less than say something wrong. When it is not sure of the exact type, it steps back to the family or the branch, like saying "a bird" when you cannot tell which bird. And when it cannot say even that, it says why, so you know whether to measure more or look closer.

    Part 3 · The model's judgement · Step 6 of 6

    Where the cell lands on the map

    In plain words

    The map is where everything comes together: example cells, the file's cells and our cell, in one place. Where a cell lands shows what it resembles, and its neighbours lend it a few more clues about its state. The answer, the reason for no answer, the best guess, the position and these clues all go back in the results file.

    For the curious: our cell's results file

    This is exactly what the service sends back for our cell, in a format called JSON. The clues are averages over the nearest example cells, weighted by how alike they are, with their spread.

    Testing VivOME

    In plain words

    Three tests, from easiest to hardest: single cells straight after training, practice files where we know every answer, and a real protein file where the types are known. VivOME does far better than guessing on the first, is right nearly every time it answers on the second, and on the real file names more cells correctly than any other method tested. That file also helped build VivOME, so it is not a fully independent test.

    Part 5 · Using VivOME today · Step 1 of 2

    Sending a file to VivOME

    In plain words

    Using VivOME online is like sending a sample to a lab that opens when you knock. The first knock after a quiet spell takes a minute to answer; then your file is read, every cell gets its answer, the results come back to you, and the lab closes again. Nothing needs installing, and the service keeps no copy of your file.

    For the curious: what comes back, and the service itself

    The download is one JSON file. For each cell it holds the answer and how precise it is (a type, a family or a branch), the shortlist, the confidence, the best guess with its chance, the reason when there is no answer, the cell's place on the map and its neighbours' clues. Its log line keeps the number of cells, how many genes matched and the share of answers, and a short fingerprint of the file, so the same file can be recognised without keeping it.

    Part 5 · Using VivOME today · Step 2 of 2

    Running VivOME yourself

    In plain words

    Running VivOME yourself is like having the lab in your own building: nothing leaves the room, and you can take bigger samples if you need to. For now the lab's key equipment, the learners, still has to be sent over by the team before it can open.

    For the curious: the two commands, and what to change

    From the top folder of a copy of the code: pip install -r service/requirements.txt, then python -m service.app. Enter its address in the Atlas page's Your data tab, under "Use your own copy of the service". Settings are read from the environment when it starts: VIVOME_MAX_UPLOAD_CELLS and VIVOME_MAX_UPLOAD_MB raise the limits, and VIVOME_ALLOWED_ORIGINS lists the pages allowed to use it: add the public site's address to use the online Atlas page with your own copy.
