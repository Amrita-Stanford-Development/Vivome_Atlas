      Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 1

      Two kinds of data

      To learn what each kind of blood cell looks like, VivOME studies examples whose kind is already known:   blood cells from Tabula Sapiens, a free public collection of human cells. Experts named each cell's type,   types in all. On the map, each dot is a cell, and cells of one type sit together.

      The types are arranged like a family tree: each type belongs to a family, such as T cells, and each family to a broad branch, such as lymphoid. When VivOME is unsure of the exact type, it can still name the family or the branch. Lit up: the branch of our cell's best guess.

      Those examples were measured by reading their RNA. The cells VivOME is used on are measured differently, by their proteins: a few thousand per cell, with many blanks and no name. The two are joined through genes, since each protein is made from a gene. Six lists of proteins from earlier experiments decide which genes are used.

      In plain words

      Every cell in the body carries the same genes, but each kind of cell switches on a different set. A switched-on gene is copied into RNA, a working message, and the RNA is used to build proteins, the molecules that do the cell's work. So reading a cell's RNA shows which genes are on, and reading its proteins shows what it has built. Both carry the gene's name, which is how VivOME lines them up.

      The cell types fall into a few broad branches. In the lymphoid branch, T cells direct the immune response or kill infected cells, NK cells kill infected or abnormal cells, and B cells and plasma cells make antibodies. In the myeloid branch, monocytes patrol the blood and become macrophages that swallow germs, dendritic cells show pieces of germs to T cells, and neutrophils are first to arrive at an infection. Red blood cells carry oxygen, stem and precursor cells make new blood cells, and platelets help blood clot.

      What a protein file looks like

      Here is a real protein file, the one our cell comes from. It is a big table, with one row per protein and one column per cell:   rows by   columns. In the picture, each tiny square is one protein in one cell: green where it was seen, pale where it was not. The rows run from the protein seen in most cells down to the rarest.

      Each number in the table says how strongly the instrument detected that protein in that cell: the bigger, the more of it. A blank means it was not detected, which often means there was too little of it to catch, not none. In all,   of this table is blank. Our cell has values for   of the   proteins.

      Every lab writes its files a little differently: some put cells in rows instead of columns, some adjust the numbers first, and each uses its own software. Before anything else, VivOME reads every kind of file into the same shape: one row per cell, one column per gene.

      In plain words

      Where the file comes from. A mass spectrometer is an instrument that breaks a cell's proteins into small pieces and weighs them very precisely; what it writes out is a raw file of signal readings, one per sample, and here each sample is one cell. DIA-NN is free software that reads those raw files and works out which proteins were there and how much of each. (Its name comes from the way the instrument is run, called data-independent acquisition or DIA, and the neural networks, NN, it uses.) Its report is the table above: a few columns describing each protein ( ), then one column per raw file, holding that protein's amount in that cell, or a blank.

      Why so many blanks. The instrument catches the most plentiful proteins in each cell and misses rarer ones, so most of the table is empty. Sometimes it cannot tell very similar proteins apart and reports them together as one protein group, which is why some names are joined with ";". Each column is named after its raw file, such as  ; VivOME keeps just the cell's name.

      Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 4

      How VivOME learns

      VivOME learns by example. It is shown the example cells, with their names, over and over: up to   rounds. After each round it adjusts itself a little, until cells of the same type land close together on its map. No protein label is used at any point.

      It practises with gaps. Protein files leave most genes blank, so during training most of each cell's genes are hidden on purpose: it sees only   to   of them, and must still recognise the cell. The cells come in mixes like real files:   of the time the natural mix, otherwise only one to eight kinds of cell.

      Several learners instead of one. The same training is run   times, each from a different random start, so each learner ends up slightly different. When a new cell arrives, all of them give their view and VivOME averages them (step 9): a group that agrees is steadier than one.

      How we know it learned well. Three designs were each trained   times and compared; this one named   of test cells correctly, counting every type equally: the best, though not by a wide margin. And one learner, trained again from scratch on this PC, matched the original closely.

      In plain words

      Learning here works like learning faces from labelled photos: after seeing enough named examples, you can name a face you have never seen. VivOME does the same with cells, learning what each type tends to look like. Its map is how it holds that knowledge: each cell becomes a point, and similar cells sit close together.

      Hiding genes on purpose is like practising to recognise a song from a few notes: it prepares VivOME for protein files, where most genes are missing. A random start is simply where a learner begins before it has learned anything; different starts end in slightly different learners, which is why several are averaged.

      For the curious: the full training recipe

      Under the hood, four rules guide each adjustment: pull each cell toward its type, pull cells of one type together, stop any single point becoming everyone's nearest neighbour, and make the two gapped views of one cell land in the same place.

      Tidying up a new file

      First, one scale. The amounts in a raw file run from small to hundreds of times larger, so most of them crowd near zero.       Next, one row per gene. A few genes appear on more than one row:   here. Their rows are joined, each cell keeping the middle of its values, so   rows become  .

      Last, the names. Each row's name is looked up on the shared list:   are found.   name several genes at once, so VivOME sets them aside rather than guess, and   are not on the list at all.

      In plain words

      Counting doublings is like a ruler marked 1, 2, 4, 8, 16: each mark is twice the one before, so a protein with a thousand times more is only about ten marks further along, not off the end of the page. It lets VivOME compare small and large amounts on an equal footing.

      Each gene needs exactly one row, because the model gives each gene one seat (step 3). And some names cannot be matched: a name that covers several genes could be any of them, and a few rows are for genes the shared list does not include. These tidy-ups happen to every file, before VivOME looks at a single cell.

      Step 9

      From a point to probabilities

      Each type has a centre on the same sphere, the average point of its reference cells. The closer the cell's point is to a centre, the higher its cosine with it.

      Each encoder turns its cosines into probabilities with a softmax at its own temperature, fitted on held-out RNA cells. A lower temperature makes them sharper.

      The five encoders' probabilities are averaged. The answer is built from this mean.

      Step 10

      Step 11

      The answer, or the reason

      The answer starts from a set: every type whose probability clears its own bar. Each bar was fitted on simulated RNA uploads to hold the true type   of the time; on protein that holds only roughly. The best guess joins any set that is not empty.

      Then a ladder. A cell with too few genes is refused first. Next, a cell unlike any reference cell: when its similarity to the nearest one, averaged over the encoders, falls below  , it is outside the reference.

      A set of one type is the answer. A set of several gives the group they share, or the lineage, and a set that shares neither is ambiguous: the cell is not given a type.

      How the results were calculated, and new data today

      First, simulated uploads: reference cells held out from training, masked like real proteomics and mixed into uploads of known make-up. Answers were right   of the time when given, and   of cells got no answer.
