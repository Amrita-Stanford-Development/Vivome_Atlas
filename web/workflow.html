How VivOME works, start to end

VivOME looks at the proteins measured in a single cell and says what kind of blood cell it is, or says plainly that it cannot tell, and why. This page shows how, one step at a time, following one real cell. You do not need a background in biology or computing: every term is explained as it comes.

About seven minutes, with captions. Space pauses, the arrow keys skip.
Start here

The question VivOME answers

  1. Every cell has a type

    Blood holds many kinds of cells: T cells and B cells that fight infection, red cells that carry oxygen, platelets that help it clot, and more. Knowing which kind a cell is comes first in almost any study of it.

  2. Two ways to read a cell

    Every cell carries the same genes but switches different ones on. A switched-on gene is copied into RNA, a working message, which is used to build proteins, the molecules that do the cell's work.

  3. One side comes with names, the other does not

    Experts have named the type of tens of thousands of cells measured by their RNA. Protein measurements of single cells are newer: they catch only the more plentiful proteins, and they rarely come with names for the cells.

  4. VivOME's idea

    Learn what each type looks like from the named RNA cells, then read a protein measurement through the genes both sides share. When the evidence is too weak to name a type, say so, and say why.

      Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 1

      Two kinds of data

      To learn what each kind of blood cell looks like, VivOME studies examples whose kind is already known:   blood cells from Tabula Sapiens, a free public collection of human cells. Experts named each cell's type,   types in all. On the map, each dot is a cell, and cells of one type sit together.

      The types are arranged like a family tree: each type belongs to a family, such as T cells, and each family to a broad branch, such as lymphoid. When VivOME is unsure of the exact type, it can still name the family or the branch. Lit up: the branch of our cell's best guess.

      Those examples were measured by reading their RNA. The cells VivOME is used on are measured differently, by their proteins: a few thousand per cell, with many blanks and no name. The two are joined through genes, since each protein is made from a gene. Six lists of proteins from earlier experiments decide which genes are used.

      In plain words

      Every cell in the body carries the same genes, but each kind of cell switches on a different set. A switched-on gene is copied into RNA, a working message, and the RNA is used to build proteins, the molecules that do the cell's work. So reading a cell's RNA shows which genes are on, and reading its proteins shows what it has built. Both carry the gene's name, which is how VivOME lines them up.

      The cell types fall into a few broad branches. In the lymphoid branch, T cells direct the immune response or kill infected cells, NK cells kill infected or abnormal cells, and B cells and plasma cells make antibodies. In the myeloid branch, monocytes patrol the blood and become macrophages that swallow germs, dendritic cells show pieces of germs to T cells, and neutrophils are first to arrive at an infection. Red blood cells carry oxygen, stem and precursor cells make new blood cells, and platelets help blood clot.

      Here is a real protein file, the one our cell comes from. It is a big table, with one row per protein and one column per cell:   rows by   columns.       Every lab writes its files a little differently: some put cells in rows instead of columns, some adjust the numbers first, and each uses its own software. Before anything else, VivOME reads every kind of file into the same shape: one row per cell, one column per gene.

      In plain words

      Where the file comes from. A mass spectrometer is an instrument that breaks a cell's proteins into small pieces and weighs them very precisely; what it writes out is a raw file of signal readings, one per sample, and here each sample is one cell. DIA-NN is free software that reads those raw files and works out which proteins were there and how much of each. (Its name comes from the way the instrument is run, called data-independent acquisition or DIA, and the neural networks, NN, it uses.) Its report is the table above: a few columns describing each protein ( ), then one column per raw file, holding that protein's amount in that cell, or a blank.

      Why so many blanks. The instrument catches the most plentiful proteins in each cell and misses rarer ones, so most of the table is empty. Sometimes it cannot tell very similar proteins apart and reports them together as one protein group, which is why some names are joined with ";". Each column is named after its raw file, such as  ; VivOME keeps just the cell's name.

      Part 1 · What the model learns · Step 4

      How VivOME learns

      VivOME learns by example. It is shown the example cells, with their names, over and over: up to   rounds. After each round it adjusts itself a little, until cells of the same type land close together on its map. No protein label is used at any point.

      It practises with most genes hidden. A real protein file has values for only a small share of the genes: our cell has   of the  . So in practice, most of each example's genes are hidden on purpose. It sees only   to   of them, and must still name the cell.

      Several learners instead of one. The same training is run   times, each from a different random start, so each learner ends up slightly different. When a new cell arrives, all of them give their view and VivOME averages them (step 8): a group that agrees is steadier than one.

      Did it learn? It was tested on example cells it had never practised on, with most of their genes hidden. It named   of them correctly, counting every type equally. Guessing at random would be right about once in   tries.

      In plain words

      Learning here works like learning faces from labelled photos: after seeing enough named examples, you can name a face you have never seen. VivOME does the same with cells, learning what each type tends to look like. Its map is how it holds that knowledge: each cell becomes a point, and similar cells sit close together.

      Hiding genes on purpose is like practising to recognise a song from a few notes: it prepares VivOME for protein files, where most genes are missing. Practice batches also come in different mixes, from a natural blood sample to a single sorted kind of cell, because real files come both ways. A random start is simply where a learner begins before it has learned anything; different starts end in slightly different learners, which is why several are averaged.

      For the curious: the full training recipe

      Under the hood, four rules guide each adjustment: pull each cell toward its type, pull cells of one type together, stop any single point becoming everyone's nearest neighbour, and make the two gapped views of one cell land in the same place.

      Part 2 · Reading a new file · Step 5

      Fitting the file to the shared list

      VivOME has one seat for each of the   genes on its shared list (step 3). It seats the file's proteins by their gene: this file fills   seats, and our cell has a value in   of them. The rest of our cell's seats stay empty.

      An empty seat is not a zero: it means "not measured", not "none there". So beside the amounts, VivOME keeps a second row for each cell, called a mask: 1 where the cell was measured, 0 where it was not.

      Too little to judge. A cell needs values in at least   genes to be read. In this file a typical cell has  . Cells that fall short get no answer:   here.

      Last, VivOME compares each gene within the file. Machines differ: one may read every protein brighter than another. So for each gene it asks whether a cell has more or less than the file's average cell, which gives the same picture whichever machine made the file.

      In plain words

      An empty seat is not a zero. Think of a form where a blank box is marked "not answered" instead of being filled in with 0: the mask is that mark, so VivOME never mistakes "not measured" for "none".

      Comparing within the file works like grading on a curve. Each cell's amount of a gene is read as above or below the file's typical cell, so a machine that reads everything brighter gives the same picture as one that reads dimmer.

      For the curious: the numbers behind it

      For each gene, the file's values are shifted and stretched so that their average is 0 and their spread is 1 (a z-score). An empty seat is set to 0 with its mask 0, so the model can tell an empty seat from an average one. The minimum of   genes is provisional.

      Part 2 · Reading a new file · Step 6

      Borrowing from similar cells

      Measuring single cells is noisy: a protein can be missed or misread by chance. So VivOME lets each cell borrow a little from the cells in the same file that look most like it. Our cell's   closest matches are linked to it here.

      Closer matches count more. Each of our cell's values becomes a blend:   its own value and   the average of its matches, with the closest matches weighing the most.

      Borrowing does not invent a measurement. A seat that was empty for our cell can now get a value from its matches, but its mask still says 0: VivOME still knows our cell did not measure that gene.

      In plain words

      It is like asking a few people who saw the same thing: one may misremember a detail, but several similar accounts steady the story. VivOME borrows only from cells in the same file, and only from the ones most like our cell, so a cell is never blended with cells of a different kind.

      For the curious: how the matches are found

      The matches are found from all of the file's proteins, not only the shared genes, in a summary of   numbers per cell. Each blended value is   the cell's own and   its matches' weighted average; the weights favour the closest matches. The table under the figure lists our cell's matches and their weights.

      Which types is our cell close to?

      Each of the   types has a home on the map: the middle of its example cells. Each learner measures how close our cell sits to every home.

      Each learner then turns closeness into chances that add up to 100%, giving most of the chance to the nearest homes.

      The   learners' chances are averaged into one. The rest of the answer is built on this average.

      In plain words

      It is like asking five people to point to where a stranger is from: each points a little differently, and the average of their answers is steadier than any single one.

      For the curious: closeness and chances

      Closeness is the cosine between the cell's point and each type's centre. Each learner turns its cosines into chances with a softmax at its own sharpness (its temperature), fitted on RNA cells it had not trained on. The table under the figure lists the average chance for every type.

      Part 3 · The model's judgement · Step 10

      The answer, or the reason for none

      VivOME only names a type when it is sure enough. Each type has its own bar to clear, set so that the true type clears it   of the time in tests on RNA cells. The types that clear their bars make a shortlist, and the likeliest type joins any shortlist that is not empty.

      Two checks come first. Was enough measured (at least   genes, step 5)? And does the cell resemble anything VivOME learned from? A cell too unlike every example cell gets no answer rather than a forced one.

      Then the shortlist decides. One type: VivOME names it. Several types in one family: it names the family. Several families in one branch: the branch. Types from different branches, or an empty shortlist: no answer.

      Across the whole file,   of   cells got an answer:   named as one type,   narrowed to a family and   to a branch. The rest got none, each with its reason.

      In plain words

      VivOME would rather say less than say something wrong. When it is not sure of the exact type, it steps back to the family or the branch, like saying "a bird" when you cannot tell which bird. And when it cannot say even that, it says why, so you know whether to measure more or look closer.
